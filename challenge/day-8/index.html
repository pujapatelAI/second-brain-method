the system: client onboarding

onboarding isn't a welcome email — that was yesterday's handoff. it's the whole first week, and a system is the same path through it every time, so it never depends on how busy you are the week they start. these questions don't audit a checklist — they trace the process: what happens the moment a client's ready, then what, then what. put a real number on each and the gaps show themselves.

1
when a client's ready to start, what gets set up on your end — and what kicks it off? their workspace, tools, access, tracker, first tasks — provisioned before day one. does signing trigger the setup, or do you scramble to build it while they're already waiting?signed → fully set up on your end: same day / ___ days later
2
what do you hand them to step in — and how do they know how to use it and reach you? access, where everything lives, how to work with you, what happens first — handed over in one clear orientation, or pieced together as they ask?their first week — how many "how do i / where is / what's next" messages do you field? ___
3

the system on one line

same path every time — that's what turns a first-week scramble into a clean start:

triggera new client is ready to start — signed, welcomed, kickoff booked (day 7 done).
inputyour setup checklist, their access + orientation, your "how we work" note (comms + what you each need).
processset up your end (workspace, tools, access, tracker, first tasks) → hand them access + show them around → lock in how you'll communicate + what you each need.
outputset up on both ends, the client oriented and confident, everyone clear on how you'll work — ready to actually begin.
standardevery new client's first week runs the same calm way, no matter how busy that week is.

the 3 mistakes in the system

1 — treating onboarding as one welcome email. one "so excited to work together!" and then silence isn't onboarding — it's a greeting. onboarding is the whole first week: your setup, their orientation, the working rhythm. a single email leaves them guessing what happens next and quietly wondering if they made a mistake. the fix is to run the first week as a system, not send a note and disappear.

2 — never setting communication norms. if you don't tell them how and when to reach you — and how often they'll hear back — they decide: a text at 9pm expecting an instant reply, a "just checking in?" three hours after the last one. you quietly resent it, and it was avoidable. set the channel, the hours, the response time, and the update cadence once, up front.

3 — rebuilding the setup from scratch every client. same workspace, same tracker, same access, same first tasks — done a little differently every time because it lives in your head. that's the ceiling on how many clients you can carry, and it's where things slip through. make the setup identical every time and the first week stops eating your week.

fix these three — run the whole first week, set the rules up front, use the same setup every time — and onboarding stops being a scramble and becomes the proof they chose right.

build your onboarding kit

one onboarding kit you reuse for every client — so the first week runs on templates, not memory. two parts. first, the your-end setup checklist — the same things set up before every client's day one:

·
workspace / project home — folders, the plan, where the work lives
·
tools & access — accounts created, permissions granted, logins ready
·
the tracker — status, tasks, and what's next, visible to you both
·
first tasks staged — the work is teed up, not starting from a blank page

then the onboarding note — the one message that orients them and sets how you'll work, sent as they start:

the onboarding note you're all set up, [name] — here's everything you need. your project lives here: [link] — that's where you'll find the plan, files, and progress. fastest way to reach me is [channel]; i reply within [time], [hours]. you'll get an update from me every [cadence], so you never have to wonder where things stand. to keep us moving i'll need [what you need] by [when]. first thing on your side: [their one next action]. really glad you're here.

build the kit once — the setup checklist and the onboarding note — and every client's first week runs the same way, whether you built it today or six months ago.

do it with Claude — two speeds

this is the video-3 payoff: once the kit exists (the setup checklist, the onboarding note, your project template), Claude runs the whole first week for you — at whichever speed you're ready for.

speed one — you're still driving. you tell Claude "[client] just signed — onboard them." from that one line it sets up their project space and tracker the way you do every client, drafts the onboarding note in your voice — where things live, how you'll communicate, what you need from them — and hands you their first-week task list. you skim, approve, send. signed to fully onboarded before you've finished your coffee.

speed two — you don't even see it. once you trust it, the moment a client is marked started the whole first week fires on its own — space built, access set, note sent, tasks staged, the update cadence scheduled. you find out it worked when the client says "this is the most organized onboarding i've ever been through."

Claude runs the onboarding you built — it doesn't invent it. the kit is what makes the setup identical every time and the automation safe. build it first (the questions above), hand it over second.
