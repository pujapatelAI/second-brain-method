revision & feedback handling

day 16, the work passed its check and went to the client. now they've reacted — and this is where good projects quietly become never-ending ones. today isn't about editing faster. it's the move before you touch the work: sorting what they said, so a revision stays a revision instead of a new project you do for free.

a revision loop is a sorting problem, not a doing problem

a project spirals into version 9 not because the client is difficult or the edits are hard — but because every reaction gets treated the same. a typo, a vague vibe, and a brand-new idea land in one pile and all get done, for free. the fix: before you touch the work, sort every note.

two piles, always. a fix is something you already agreed to — it's in the brief, you just do it. a new request is something they thought of after — not wrong to want, but it's a change, not a revision. the sentence that saves the project: "happy to — that's a little outside what we scoped; here's what it'd take." said kindly, said early.

the note you can't sort yet is the vague one. "make it pop," "not quite it" — a feeling, not a change. act on it and you're guessing: revise, miss, burn a round. turn it concrete first — one question back — then revise once.

then the version goes back with each note answered one by one — what changed, and what didn't and why. they see they were heard and re-review only what moved. that's what turns round 4 into round 2.

the system: four questions

answer these before your next revision, and "handling feedback" stops being a scramble and becomes a process. write your answers in.

1
the one place it lands — when a client reacts, where does the feedback go? one place tied to that deliverable, or scattered across call, email, doc, and text? scattered means you revise from memory — and memory drops the quiet notes.on your last deliverable, how many separate places did feedback come in from — 1, 3, 5? ___
2
the sort — is each note a "fix" or a "new request," sorted before you start? a fix is in the brief, you owe it; a new request is a change. that sort is the whole line between a revision and unpaid scope creep.of the notes on your last revision, how many were fixing what you promised vs brand-new — ___ fix / ___ new?
3
the translate — a vague reaction isn't a change yet. concrete it before revising, or guess? "make it pop" is a feeling, not an instruction — turn it into a specific change first, usually with one question back.out of last round's notes, how many could you act on as written vs had to ask "what do you mean" — ___ of ___?
4
the close + the count — does the revision go back with a what-changed note, and how many rounds does a project get? each note → what changed, or why it's separate. and is the round limit written down, or discovered when you're already annoyed?on your last project, how many rounds did it take vs how many did you plan for — 1, 2, 5, lost count? ___

the system on one line

turn a pile of reactions into one clean revised version — without the project reopening every time:

triggerthe deliverable passed its check (day 16), went to the client, and their feedback comes back.
inputevery piece of feedback pulled into one place, next to the original brief (day 9) and the deliverable (day 15).
processgather it all in one place → sort each note: in-scope fix vs new request → turn every vague note into a concrete change before you touch the work → make the edits → send it back with what-changed, note by note.
outputone clean revised version and a client who can see they were heard — not a pile of half-tracked versions and a project that reopened itself.
standardrevisions are bounded (name the number in the contract, day 7): in-scope notes get fixed, new requests become their own conversation, every round closes with a what-changed note → then it moves to sign-off (day 18).

the 3 mistakes in the system

1 — the feedback never lands in one place. notes come in on a call, an email, a text, a doc comment — and you revise from memory, not one list. you fix the loud notes, miss the quiet ones, and the next round reopens what was done.

2 — every note gets treated as in-scope. no line between "fix what we agreed on" and "new idea they just had," so every reaction becomes free work and the project has no end. nothing tells you which notes you actually owe.

3 — you act on vague feedback instead of translating it. you guess at what they meant, revise, and it's still wrong — a whole round on a misread. the unclear note has to become concrete before you touch the work.

fix these three and the edits were never the problem — the pile was. sort it first and a round gets short, finite, and closeable.

do it with Claude — on my real delivery process

the reason feedback from twelve consulting clients, a group program, a software, and live workshops never spirals is that i don't sort it in my head — i run it through my company brain in Claude Code. same premise as every day: you build the rules first — the brief, the scope, what's a fix vs a change — so the AI knows how to sort what comes back.

1
i pull every note into one place. email, call transcript, doc comments → one change list tied to that deliverable, deduped, nothing lost in a thread.
2
it tags each note against the brief — in-scope fix vs new request. so the "this is a change, let's talk" flag comes up before i revise, not after i've done it for free. that tag is the whole scope-creep defense.
3
vague notes → a question in my voice ("make it pop — headline or color?"), so nothing's revised on a guess. and it drafts the send-back note: every piece of feedback → what i changed, or why it's separate.

the line you keep both hands on: Claude sorts and drafts the loop — i decide what's a favor i eat vs a change i charge for, and i do the revision. second set of eyes on the pile, never the one deciding what's free. the scope call and the fix are what they pay you for — you hand over the sorting because you defined the rules first.
