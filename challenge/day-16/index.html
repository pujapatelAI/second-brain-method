The 30-Day Brain Extract · Day 16 · Phase 2 — Delivering the Work

quality-control checklist

last time, you drafted the work fast. today, before it goes anywhere, it clears the same check every time. and here's the thing nobody names out loud: quality isn't a trait, it's a system. when the check lives in your head, the work comes out as good as the day you were having — sharp when you're rested, sloppy when you're slammed. the whole point of today is to make "is this good enough" stop being a feeling.

quality can't be a personality trait

the reason work slips out with a typo, a dead link, or the last client's name still in the header isn't that you're careless. it's that the check was never a thing — it was you, remembering to look, on a day you had the time. that makes quality a coin flip against your calendar. the fix is boring and it's the whole game: a written list the work runs every time, so the standard stops depending on your mood.

the second half, the one most people skip: a checklist has two layers. the universal checks are true of everything you send — right name, links open, dates match what you promised, nothing left over from a past version. the specific checks change per kind of work — a report, an ad, a contract each have their own. one list for what never changes, one list per thing you make.

and the check that actually saves the relationship isn't the typo check — it's the brief check. "is this correct" catches the cheap mistakes. "does this do the job we agreed on" catches the expensive ones. clean-and-wrong ships more easily than obviously-rough, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. so the last thing every deliverable does before it moves is get held up against the original promise — not just proofread.

the system: four questions

answer these before your next deliverable leaves your hands, and "did i check it" turns from a shrug into a yes. write your answers in.

1
the universal bar — what has to be true of everything you send, no matter what it is? the checks that don't change whether it's an invoice, a report, or an ad: right name on it, every link opens, dates match what you promised, nothing left over from the last client's version. write it down once and it stops depending on whether you remembered to look.how many items would be on that list right now — 0, 3, 10? ___
2
the specific bar — pick the one thing you deliver most. what checks matter for that that wouldn't apply to anything else? a report, an ad, a contract each carry their own. the checklist isn't one list — it's one per thing you make, so the question has a real answer instead of a shrug.name them out loud — if you get past 2, you already have a checklist in your head that's never been written down. how many? ___
3
the real check — does your list ask "is this correct" or "does this do the job we agreed on"? proofreading catches typos; only holding it against the brief catches a deliverable that's clean and still doesn't do what you promised. that's the miss that costs you the client.your last deliverable — did anyone hold it up against the original brief before it went out? yes / no ___
4
the reviewer + the trigger — who runs the check, is it different eyes than the ones that made it, and does it run every time? you can't proofread your own work at the altitude you wrote it — you read what you meant, not what's there. a second person is ideal; when it's just you, the written checklist is the second set of eyes. and it has to fire on the rushed weeks too, not only when you remember.out of your last 5 deliverables, how many got a real second look before they shipped? ___ / 5

the system on one line

turn "i think it's fine" into a bar every deliverable clears the same way:

triggera deliverable is drafted (day 15) and about to leave your hands.
inputthe draft, the original brief (day 9), and your checklist — the universal list plus the one for this kind of work.
processrun the universal list → run the this-type list → hold it against the brief, not just for typos → different eyes, even if the eyes are the checklist itself.
outputnothing leaves your hands you'd be embarrassed for the client to catch — because the check happened, not because you happened to be careful that day.
standardevery deliverable of a given type clears the same written bar before it moves to sign-off (day 18) — and each new mistake becomes a new line on the list.

the 3 mistakes in the system

1 — it lives in your head, not on paper. so the check only runs on the calm days and vanishes on the slammed ones — which is exactly when the mistakes happen. quality ends up being a function of how your week went instead of a property of the work. write the list down and it runs the same whether you're fresh or fried.

2 — it checks the surface but never the brief. typos caught, formatting clean, links all live — and the thing still doesn't do what you promised. clean-and-wrong ships more easily than obviously-rough, because nothing looks off. every check has to end by asking "does this deliver what we agreed on," not just "is this tidy."

3 — it never gets updated. every mistake that slips through should become a new line on the list. when it doesn't, the checklist keeps catching last year's problems and stays blind to the one that just cost you. a checklist that doesn't grow, rots — the moment something gets past you is the moment it earns a permanent row.

fix these three and quality stops being something you hope you had the energy for. it becomes the one thing you can promise every client, on every deliverable, no matter what your week looked like.

do it with Claude — on my real delivery process

the only way i run twelve consulting clients, a group program, a software, and live in-person workshops without quality slipping is that the check isn't in my head — it's built into my company brain in Claude Code. same premise as every day of this challenge: you build the checklist, the rules, and the guardrails first, so the AI knows exactly what "good" means to you. here's how the QC one actually runs.

1
i built the checklist once by letting Claude interview me. what's gone wrong before, what i always check, what each kind of deliverable needs — it asked, i answered, and it wrote the list down. now it lives on paper instead of in my memory, which is the whole difference between a system and a good intention.
2
before anything ships, i hand it the draft, the original brief, and the checklist. it runs every item and tells me exactly what failed and where — the dead link, the name mismatch, the line that drifted from what we promised. the against-the-brief catch is the one that earns its keep: "you promised X, the draft delivers Y." a proofreader sails right past that; the brief check stops it.
3
the list gets sharper every time. when something slips past me, i tell Claude once and it adds the row — so the check compounds instead of going stale. every mistake i make is the last time i make that one, because it's now a permanent line the AI runs on every future deliverable.

and here's the line you keep both hands on: Claude runs the check and shows me what failed — i make the call on what actually goes out. it's the second set of eyes, never the sign-off. the judgment about whether the work is truly ready is the thing they pay you for — you only get to hand over the looking because you defined what to look for first.

that's the guardrail on this whole system: the checklist catches; you clear it. (video 3 above walks the whole thing.)
