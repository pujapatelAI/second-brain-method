The 30-Day Brain Extract · Day 15 · Phase 2 — Delivering the Work

deliverable drafting

everything so far was the wrapper — onboarding, the brief, the plan, the tasks, the meetings. today you make the thing: the report, the strategy, the audit, the copy, whatever the client is actually paying for. and here's what nobody tells you — the deliverable is the most repeatable thing you do, not the least. the blank page isn't a creativity problem. it's a missing-system problem.

the deliverable is the one thing people refuse to systematize

because they think it's the creative part — the sacred core that can't be templated. it's the opposite. same kind of client, same kind of deliverable, every time: the same structure, the same voice, the same quality bar. what feels like a blank page every time isn't missing inspiration — it's a missing starting structure.

and the second half, the one that unblocks you: a first draft is not supposed to be right. its only job is to be complete enough to react to. the people who stare at the blank page are trying to make the first draft the final draft in one pass — so they never start. get to a wrong-but-whole v1 fast, and you're editing instead of inventing.

which means every deliverable splits in two: the ~80% that's assembly — the structure, the client's own words from the brief, the calls made in your meetings, the parts you've written a hundred times — and the ~20% that's actually your expertise, the judgment only you can make. the whole system is: stop rebuilding the 80%, so all your time goes to the 20% that's the reason they hired you.

the system: three questions

answer these before you make your next deliverable, and the blank page turns into a process. write your answers in.

1
what tells you it's time to start drafting — and when you sit down, what do you start from: a blank page, or a structure you reuse? if it's blank every time, that's the whole problem. the same deliverable should start from the same skeleton — your sections, your order, your standard — so you're never inventing the container, only filling it.the last deliverable you made — did you start it from a template, or from nothing? ___
2
how much of the draft is you assembling what's already decided — the goal in their words, the scope, the calls from your meetings — versus the part that's actually your expertise? name that line. the first part is assembly; it shouldn't cost you a single new thought. the second part is the only part that needs your brain.of your last deliverable, what % was you re-writing things already decided vs. real analysis? ___
3
what does a finished first draft actually contain, how do you know it's done enough to react to, and where does it go next? a draft's job is to be reacted to, not to be right. decide what "complete enough to send myself or a teammate" means — and where it goes from there (tomorrow: the quality check).what's your line for "this draft is ready for me to edit" — is it written down anywhere? ___

the system on one line

turn a blank page into a first draft you can react to today:

triggera deliverable task is up (from day 11) and the inputs exist.
inputyour standard structure for this deliverable type — plus the brief (day 9), the client's own words, and the decisions from your meetings (day 14).
processstart from the skeleton, not zero → pour in everything already decided → spend your expertise only on the 20% that's yours → stop at a complete, wrong-but-whole v1.
outputa real first draft you can react to today — made in a fraction of the time, because you weren't inventing the container or re-deciding what was decided.
standardevery deliverable of a given type starts from the same structure and ends at a defined "ready to edit" line — before it ever gets checked (day 16) or sent.

the 3 mistakes in the system

1 — you start every one from a blank page. same kind of deliverable, and you rebuild the whole thing from scratch every time — structure, sections, setup — like you've never made one before. that's not craft, it's waste. build the skeleton once, and every future draft starts half-done.

2 — you rewrite what was already decided. the goal in their words, the scope, the calls made in your meetings — it's all already captured, and you retype it from memory anyway, usually slightly different from what you actually agreed. the assembly shouldn't cost you a single new thought. pull it in; save your brain for the part that's yours.

3 — the draft has no finish line, so it has nowhere to go. you never set what "done" means for a draft, so there's no line between a first pass and the final thing — and no defined next step. so it either gets polished into the ground, or the raw v1 goes straight to the client with no checkpoint in between. a draft gets its own "ready to react to" line, and a next stop (your quality check — tomorrow), not a send button.

fix these three and the deliverable stops eating your nights — you start it further along, finish it faster, and it comes out more consistent than when you did every inch by hand.

do it with Claude — on my real audit process

the only way i handle twelve consulting clients, a group program, a software, and live in-person company workshops is that i have a company brain in Claude Code handling a lot of the back-end. and the reason it works is the whole premise of this challenge: you build the systems, the rules, and the guardrails so the AI knows exactly what you want. here's a real one.

one of my main consulting deliverables is the audit, and the process has four stages: interview all the relevant people → a full data review → the analysis → presenting the solution. the interviews are the input to everything after — and for me to run the analysis, i need the output of each interview structured a certain way. that certain way is a framework i've defined, so i know exactly how i want it — which means Claude knows how i want it too.

1
the framework is the guardrail. because the structure is defined, the moment any interview meeting is complete, it gets added into the context bank and put into the exact output i analyze from. i'm not reformatting notes — the drafted, structured input is just there, in my shape, ready.
2
it's not just faster — it's more accurate. anything i might have missed by listening again or scrubbing a transcript is caught, because all the context is captured against the framework. and because every point is traceable, if i want to go ask a client a question, i can — i know it came from a specific interview.
3
the compounding part. speeding up the audit means clients are happier that they get results faster, i can take on more clients, and over time i can charge a premium for the fact that we deliver this faster — without dropping quality.

notice what the AI is doing and not doing: it structures the inputs so i can think — the analysis and the solution are still mine. that's the line. you don't hand over the judgment they pay you for. you hand over the busywork around it — and you only get to hand it over because you did it by hand first and defined the framework.

this is the one to keep both hands on: the deliverable is the product. AI gets you off the blank page — it doesn't get to ship your thinking. (video 3 above walks the whole thing.)
