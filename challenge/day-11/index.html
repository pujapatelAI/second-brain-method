The 30-Day Brain Extract · Day 11 · Phase 2 — Delivering the Work

task breakdown & assignment

day 10 gave you the timeline — when everything has to happen. today every line on it becomes somebody's, with a date and a definition of done. because a task with no name and no date on it isn't assigned. it's a wish.

wait — didn't day 10 already tell me to list the tasks?

it did, and it's a fair question. here's the line between them.

the plan is a promise you made to yourself. the assignment is a promise somebody made to you.

day 10 is about the project — does the shape of this thing land by the date. day 11 is about monday morning — does anything actually move without you standing over it.

the fastest way to feel the difference: you can have a perfect timeline, correctly sequenced, properly buffered — and still be doing every single line of it yourself at 11pm. the plan told you whether you'd make it. the assignment is what makes it move.

the system: every line gets a seat, a date, and a done

walk the tasks you already sorted yesterday, and put three things on each one. write your answers in.

1
is each piece small enough to actually start? if you can't picture someone opening their laptop and finishing it in one sitting, it isn't a task yet — it's still a milestone. "redesign the website" is a wish; "write the copy for the home page" is a task.your biggest task — can it be finished in one sitting? ___
2
who owns it — and name the seat, not just the person. write the role first — "the editor," "whoever does intake," "the client" — then put a person in it. people change; the work shouldn't fall on the floor when they do.seats in your business, written down: ___
3
when is it due — and how does that person find out it's theirs? if the assignment only happened in your head, or in a message they scrolled past, it didn't happen. it lives in one place they check.where does an assignment land for your team? ___
4
what does done look like, and where do you both see status? agree on done up front — otherwise it comes back half-right and you redo it yourself, which is how you end up doing everyone's job.one place status lives: ___

you have exactly four seats

every task in your business is owned by one of four, and no more:

youthe calls only you can make, and the work you're genuinely best at.
your teama role, not a name — the editor, the VA, whoever does intake.
the clientthe things they hand over. these belong on the plan with dates, same as anyone's.
AIthe lines nothing about you needs to be doing.

and if it's just you right now, this is the one that matters most. you're sitting in all four seats. writing them down as seats is the entire reason you'll be able to hand one off later — without having to explain your whole business to somebody first.

the system on one line

take yesterday's timeline and make every line somebody's:

triggerthe plan's set, the work's about to start.
inputthe sorted, dated tasks already sitting in your day-10 plan.
processcut each milestone into one-sitting pieces → give every piece a seat and a person in it → date it and put it where they'll see it → agree what done means → one place you both check.
outputyou can see what's moving and what's stuck without asking a single person for an update.
standardno line is assigned until it has a seat, a date, and a done.

what it looks like finished

one milestone off a real plan — videos filmed & edited, landing friday oct 9. four lines, four seats, four finish lines. the shaded one is where the timeline leaves your hands:

film the six scripts client due fri sep 25 done = six raw files in the shared folder load-bearing — everything behind this moves if it moves
cut six verticals + captions editor due fri oct 2 done = six exports, captions burned in
review and give notes strategist due wed oct 7 done = notes given once, not drip-fed
apply notes, final export editor due fri oct 9 done = final files named to spec

notice what you can tell at a glance: whose desk it's on, whether it's late, and what "finished" means — without asking anyone. that's the whole output of this system.

the 3 mistakes in the system

1 — the task is too big to start. "handle onboarding." "fix the funnel." nobody knows what to open on monday morning, so it sits. if you can't picture someone sitting down and finishing it in one go, it isn't a task yet — and a task that can't be started won't be.

2 — you assign to a person instead of a seat. everything lives as "that's Sarah's" — then Sarah's out, and the work strands, because nobody wrote down that someone has to do intake. they wrote down Sarah. name the role, put a person in it; the day someone changes, you fill a seat instead of rebuilding a system.

3 — you assign the work but keep the tracking in your head. no due date, no shared place to look, no agreed "done" — so the only way to know anything is to go ask. you handed off the work and kept the mental load, which is the one thing you actually needed off your plate.

fix these three and you stop chasing people. the work has a name, a date, and a place — and stuck things raise their own hand.

do it with Claude

the plan already exists from yesterday — so the assigning is the easy part to hand over.

it takes each milestone and cuts it into real tasks, every one starting with a verb and small enough to finish in a sitting. then it puts an owner on each: yours, your team's, the client's, or the ones it can just do itself. it dates them backwards from the milestone, flags every task waiting on the client, and drafts the handoff message in your voice — what it is, what done looks like, when it's due. you read it, change one owner, approve.

and then the part that actually gives you your week back: you never chase it. it tells you what's outstanding and whose desk it's sitting on, before you'd have thought to ask.
