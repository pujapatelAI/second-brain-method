The 30-Day Brain Extract · Day 14 · Phase 2 — Delivering the Work

meeting notes → action items

yesterday's agenda made the meeting worth having — you walked in with the decisions that had to get made. today is the other half: walking out with them captured. because a meeting isn't done when you hang up. it's done when every decision and every next step is written down and owned — otherwise the best call of your week quietly evaporates by friday, and you spend more time figuring out what you were supposed to do than actually doing it.

notes are a receipt. action items are the work.

here's the trap: you have a genuinely good meeting — aligned, decisions made, everyone nodding — and a week later nothing's moved. it's not your memory. the meeting produced something, and nobody caught it.

everybody bought an AI notetaker to fix this, and nothing changed — because notes were never the bottleneck. a wall of transcript is a receipt that the meeting happened. it is not a record of what has to happen next. the whole job is pulling the few real decisions and the owned next steps out of the talk.

which gives you the one rule this system runs on: capture decisions and owned actions — never the conversation. a decision nobody wrote down gets re-litigated next week like it never happened; a next step with no owner and no date isn't an action, it's a wish. the meeting's whole value is the 5% you extract and route somewhere real.

the system: three questions

the whole capture comes out of three questions. answer them the moment a meeting ends and nothing decided in it disappears. write your answers in.

1
where do your meeting notes actually live? in your head, a scattered doc, or a transcript you never reopen? if the meeting only lives somewhere you'll never look at again, it might as well not have been captured. pick one findable place — that's the whole difference between a system and a graveyard.your last meeting — where are its notes right now? could you find the decisions in 30 seconds? ___
2
what actually got decided? not everything that was said — the decisions. "we're moving the launch to the 17th," "we're going with option B." capture the decision, not the discussion, so nobody reopens a settled call next week.name one decision from your last call — is it written anywhere, or only in your memory? ___
3
which action items are you actually doing — and once you decide, who owns each one, by when, and where does it land? of everything floated, commit to the few that are real. then every one gets an owner (a seat, not just a name — you, your team, the client, or AI, from day 11), a date, and a place it lands: where your tasks already live.your last meeting created how many "someone should…" items — how many have a name and a date on them today? ___

the system on one line

turn a finished conversation into work that's already moving:

triggerthe meeting ends.
inputthe recording or your notes — plus last meeting's open items, so you can check what actually got done.
processpull the decisions → commit to the real action items → put an owner and a date on each → route them where your work lives (day 11) → send a short recap to the room.
outputnothing decided gets re-litigated, nothing agreed-to falls on the floor, and a written record of who owns what by when.
standardevery meeting ends with a recap sent and the actions in your tracker — before the next thing starts. no recap, and the meeting may as well not have happened.

what it looks like finished

the recap from the same Riverside Lending check-in you built the agenda for yesterday — sent within the hour, straight off the call. notice it's three things, not a transcript: what got decided, who owns what by when, and what's still open. and it closes the exact loop the agenda opened — "confirm who's sending the access and by when" now has a name and a date on it.

that recap took two minutes and closed the loop yesterday's agenda opened. nothing from the call is sitting in anyone's memory.

the 3 mistakes in the system

1 — you capture notes, not decisions and actions. you (or your notetaker) save a wall of everything that was said — and a transcript nobody rereads is a receipt, not a record. next week nobody scrolls it. pull the decisions and the owned next steps out; that's the 5% the meeting was actually for.

2 — the next steps leave with no owner and no date. "someone should follow up on that" — everyone nods, no name, no day, and it dies in the hallway. every action gets a person, a date, and a place it lands before the call ends, or it isn't an action — it's a wish.

3 — the capture never leaves the notes doc. you wrote it down perfectly… into a document that isn't where your work lives, so it's invisible until it's already overdue. it only counts if it lands in your tracker and the room gets the recap — otherwise you did the work of remembering and got none of the benefit.

fix these three and you stop having the same meeting twice — what gets decided gets done, and you stop spending more time figuring out what to do than doing it.

do it with Claude — teach it once, then let it run

we've all had the day of back-to-back meetings where every call spits out action items — and you don't have time to do them, let alone figure out which ones are even worth your time. that's the whole premise of this challenge: you work the system by hand first, until you know exactly what "right" looks like — then you teach it to AI so it runs on your behalf while you're still in your next meeting. here's the build, the way i do it in Claude Code with a second brain. use whatever you use.

1
connect your source. you can't extract action items from a meeting your AI can't see. from the earlier days you already record every call and keep a transcript — Otter, Granola, whatever you use. go into Claude Code and connect that tool, so it reads every meeting on its own without you pasting anything in.
2
teach it how to extract — manually, the first few times. don't reach for automation yet. talk to it: "this is exactly what i want pulled from a meeting, and this is how i want it formatted." read what it gives back, correct it, and iterate until the output is something you'd actually use. you're not writing a clever prompt — you're teaching it your standard until it nails it every time.
3
ask it what it needs from you — to execute, not just list. here's the shift most people miss: the goal isn't to get a list of action items. it's to get them done. so try: "i just got off a meeting — identify the action items, then tell me exactly what you need from me to execute on them." now it's not reporting; it's naming the one or two things only you can give it so it can actually carry them out.
4
turn it into a routine. once you're happy with all of it, make it a routine — something that runs on a scheduled cadence, or watches for a trigger across your apps. mine watches for a meeting to end and runs 30 minutes later, on its own. be crystal clear on the trigger: the exact event that starts it, and exactly what it does when it fires. that's the line between a thing you have to remember to run and a system that just runs.

the goal was never a list of action items — anyone's notetaker can tell you what they are. yours does them. you worked it by hand, taught it your standard, and set the trigger — so now every meeting turns itself into done action items while you're already in the next one. that's the whole challenge, in one system.

one to keep a hand on: it's acting on your behalf now — a recap or anything it sends is a written record people act on, so read it before it goes, especially the first few weeks. (video 3 above walks the whole run.)
