The 30-Day Brain Extract · Day 12 · Phase 2 — Delivering the Work

the weekly status update

for four days the work has faced inward — the brief, the plan, the tasks, who owns each piece. today it turns outward. the one note your client gets every week, so they never have to ask where things stand — and never go quiet long enough to wonder if they made a mistake hiring you.

the system, the 3 mistakes that keep clients chasing you, and the whole thing written on claude every friday morning. swipe →

you don't send it because there's news

the mistake underneath every late, skipped, or over-long update is thinking the update is for reporting progress. it isn't. you send it because they're wondering — and one note a week is what buys you an uninterrupted week.

clients almost never leave over a date that moved. they leave over the three weeks they didn't hear from you and started guessing. silence is the only thing a client reads as failure.

which is why the rule that makes this system work is the one that feels pointless: you send it on the boring weeks too. "here's what moved, here's what didn't, here's why" is a completely normal update — and it's the one that keeps them calm.

the system: three questions

the whole update comes out of three questions. answer them once and you have a note you can send every friday without thinking. write your answers in.

1
what does your client actually need to know every week? four things, and only four: what got finished, what's next, what's sitting with them, and whether anything moved the date. not everything you did — where things stand.your last update — how many of the four did it actually answer? ___ / 4
2
when does it go out — and does it still go out in a week where nothing exciting happened? same day, same time, every week. the boring weeks matter most; that's exactly when they'd otherwise start guessing.your slot: same day + time, every week? ___
3
where do the facts come from? from where your tasks already live — not from you scrolling back through a week of messages trying to remember. and end with the one thing you need from them, or a plain "nothing needed this week."how long does writing it take you now? ___ (over 15 min = a day-11 problem, not a status problem)

the system on one line

take yesterday's tracker and turn it into the note they've been waiting on:

triggerit's your day, the project's live.
inputthe owned, dated tasks already sitting in your day-11 tracker.
processpull from the tracker → sort into done / next / waiting on you / anything that changed the date → say plainly whether you're on track → end with the one ask.
outputa client who never has to check in — and a written record of every week the project was sitting on their side.
standardit goes out on the slow weeks too. no update is "nothing to report" — "nothing moved, here's why" is the update.

what it looks like finished

the friday note for the same project you planned on day 10 and assigned on day 11 — Riverside Lending, paid-social launch, due nov 17. four sections, one ask, forty seconds to read:

Riverside — week of Oct 3
on track? yes, on track for the nov 17 launch. here's where things stand.
done all six videos edited and back from review.
next up building the campaign and running it against the QA checklist.
waiting on you final go on the ad-account access — still showing pending.
timeline on track for nov 17, as long as access lands this week.
this week: can you confirm the ad-account access by friday? that's the one thing holding the build.

notice what they can tell in one read: whether you're on track, what's on your plate, what's on theirs, and the one thing to do — without a single follow-up question. that's the whole output of this system.

the 3 mistakes in the system

1 — you only send one when there's good news. so your client learns that hearing from you means progress, and silence means something's wrong. now every quiet week costs you trust you didn't know you were spending. send it in the slow weeks especially — the boring update is the one that keeps them calm.

2 — it's a list of what you did, not where things stand. nobody hired you for an activity log. they have one question — are we still on track for the date — and if your update doesn't answer it in the first line, they'll ask you anyway, which is the whole thing you were trying to avoid.

3 — it ends with nothing for them to do. no ask, and no flag on what's been sitting on their side for two weeks — so the ball quietly rolls back to you, and when the date slips it looks like you were slow. name what you need and when you need it, every single week.

fix these three and "any updates?" just stops arriving.

do it with Claude — build the routine once

here's the tactical version, and it's the whole point of the challenge. don't hand Claude the task every friday and re-explain your format, your voice, and where the facts live — do that and you re-teach it every week and get a different email every time. you teach it once, then it runs on a routine — you don’t even trigger it.

1
let it interview you. tell Claude: "interview me about my weekly client update, then save it as a skill i can run every friday." it walks you through the same questions you answered above — your four sections, your slot, where the facts live, your usual ask, what your voice sounds like. you answer once.
2
it writes the skill. Claude turns your answers into a saved routine — the format, the sources, the voice, and the rules baked in ("always end with one ask," "flag anything sitting on the client's side two weeks or more"). that saved skill is the extract. you never explain it again.
3
set it to run on a routine. now it fires every friday morning on its own — no "hey Claude," you don’t have to remember it’s friday. it pulls from the tracker, your threads, and your meeting notes, and the drafts are waiting when you sit down: one note per client, in your voice, same four sections, each ending with the one thing you need from them. you skim, fix a sentence, approve.

you're not asking Claude to be clever every friday — you're not even remembering to ask. you taught it your update once, and now the routine runs it for you. the process came out of your head and into something that runs on its own — which is the entire challenge, in one system.

one to keep a hand on: a status update is a promise in writing — read it before it goes. (video 3 above walks the friday-morning run.)
