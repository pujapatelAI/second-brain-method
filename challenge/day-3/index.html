The 30-Day Brain Extract · Day 3 · Phase 1 — Getting Clients

discovery-call prep

the whole goal of a discovery call is one question, asked both ways: is this client a good fit for me, and am i a good fit for them? prep is how you walk in already knowing most of the answer — so the call is calm, in your control, and pointed at the outcome. today you build that prep structure. (this is the prep — running the call itself is day 4.)

the three questions that build your prep

1
what makes someone your ideal client — deeper than qualification? day 2 was the surface read: are they worth a call at all? this goes deeper — it's about whether to take them on. their mindset and beliefs, how the problem actually shows up for them, and what they're willing to do to fix it.a coaching client who won't do the work won't work. a service client who won't change anything about their operations won't work. get clear on how your ideal client behaves and what change they're open to.
2
what questions get them to rant about the problem you solve? in a good discovery call, they talk more than you. prep the questions that make them hyper-aware of how the problem is showing up — the symptoms.mine, since i do operations: "how organized is that right now?" / "where do you feel most overwhelmed?" — both are symptoms of bad operations. the right questions create urgency, because they say the cost out loud.
3
what do you already have on them? their linkedin, their website, past conversations, and — the big one — where they came from as a lead.lead source is the tell: a specific ad, a specific video, the offer they filled out a form for. it already points at the problem on their mind and the solution they're reaching for. pull it before you dial.

what you walk in with

do those three and you don't walk in blank — you walk in with a structure and a goal: who your ideal client is, whether this person fits, the problem they're most likely facing, and the questions that will confirm it.

and here's why prep matters more than people think: not every offer needs a proposal, so a discovery call often becomes the sales call — especially if there's nothing to send afterward. the more due diligence you've done, the more you can show them you genuinely understand their business — and that you're the right person to fix it. feeling understood is what builds the trust that gets someone to buy.

a call structure you can steal

prep produces a plan for the call. here's the shape most of mine take:

frameset it up: "this call is for us to figure out if we're a fit — both ways." lowers the pressure, puts you in control.
surfaceask the symptom questions. let them rant. listen more than you talk.
confirmtest the hypothesis you prepped — "sounds like the real issue is X, is that fair?" you're confirming, not fishing.
positionshow how you solve that exact problem. now you're the obvious fit, not a pitch.
next stepbook the build, or — if there's no proposal to send — close here. always leave with a defined next step.

symptom questions to steal

adapt these to what you solve — the point is they make the cost of the problem obvious, without you pitching:

  • walk me through how [that process] works right now — start to finish.
  • where does it break most often?
  • what happens when you step away for a week?
  • how much time a week does that eat?
  • what have you already tried, and why didn't it stick?
  • if this were fixed, what would change for you?

none of these pitch. the urgency comes from them answering — which is the whole point.

the intel checklist — and what each source tells you

·
their website → what they do, rough size, how they talk about themselves.
·
their linkedin → role, authority, tenure, how they frame their work.
·
past conversations (email / DMs) → what they've already told you — don't ask it again.
·
lead source → the ad / video / offer they came from = the problem already on their mind.
·
reviews & public info → where they're strong, where it likely hurts.

turn it into one line before the call: "this is [who], they came from [source], their likely problem is [X], and i'll confirm it by asking [question]." that one line is your whole prep.

the three mistakes in the system

1 — you prep your pitch instead of your questions. you get your offer, your deck, all the reasons you can help — but not the questions that get them into "these are real problems, and they matter." the right questions create the urgency. prep the questions, not the pitch.

2 — you don't pull the intel you already have. most people go in blind — no prep, no due diligence. but if you understand who you're talking to before you talk to them, they feel understood → they trust you → they buy.

3 — you go in with no hypothesis, so you're fishing. if you prepped right, you already have a read on their problem — from their business, industry, revenue level, and what they've told you. on the call you're confirming, not diagnosing from zero. be in control: know why you're there and how you help.

run the whole prep with claude

prep gets time-consuming at volume. so once your system exists, you turn it into a routine claude runs for you. mine:

·
goes through my google calendar and finds external people i'm meeting who aren't already clients
·
matches them to the lead intake + qualification from days 1–2 (including the offer they were triaged into)
·
runs the due diligence — past conversations, their website, their linkedin, everything
·
knows the problem hypothesis + the offer we're framing toward
·
writes a one-page brief — the exact questions to ask and the structure for the call
·
emails it to me an hour before every call, so i'm not digging through seven tools

but this only works if the system exists first — the ideal-client definition, the questions, the intel you pull, the intake + qualification from days 1–2. every system builds on the last. that's the whole point of doing them in order.
